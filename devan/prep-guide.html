Interview Prep Guide

Your Military Experience Is Your Advantage

An interview prep guide for veterans entering financial services. You already have what it takes. This guide helps you show it.

Before You Read This

You already have what it takes to succeed as a financial advisor. The problem isn't your qualifications — it's how you talk about them.

Research shows that veterans interviewing for civilian roles make three consistent mistakes:

  1. Over-explaining military context — spending most of the answer teaching the interviewer what your job was, instead of why it matters
  2. Using jargon — acronyms and military shorthand that mean nothing to a civilian hiring manager
  3. Leading with operations instead of people — talking about what you did instead of who you did it for

This guide fixes that.

Find Your Archetype

Most military experience maps to one of four archetypes. Find yours and use the translations below as starting points — not scripts. Adapt them to your actual experience.

The Combat Leader

Infantry, Armor, Special Operations, Combat Arms

You led teams in high-stakes environments. You made decisions under pressure with incomplete information. You were responsible for people's lives.

Instead of saying... Say this...
"I led a platoon of 40 Marines" "I was responsible for 40 people's outcomes in environments where the plan changed daily"
"I conducted operations in hostile territory" "I built trust with teams under real pressure — the kind where people's futures depended on my judgment"
"I briefed senior leadership" "I translated complex situations into clear recommendations for decision-makers — similar to translating a client's financial picture into an actionable plan"
Practice Question Tell me about a time you had to make a call that affected someone's future — and you didn't have all the information you wanted.

The Operations / Logistics Specialist

Supply Chain, Budget Management, Personnel, Compliance

You built and maintained systems that kept entire units functioning. You managed budgets, personnel flows, and complex organizations.

Instead of saying... Say this...
"I managed a $12M annual budget" "I was personally accountable for resource allocation decisions that affected hundreds of people — and every dollar had to be justified"
"I oversaw supply chain operations" "I built systems that ensured people got what they needed, when they needed it — even when conditions changed unexpectedly"
"I maintained 98% readiness rates" "I measured what mattered and held myself to a standard where failure wasn't abstract — it meant someone didn't have what they needed"
Practice Question Tell me about a system you built or fixed that people depended on — and what happened when it was tested.

The Human Services Professional

Medical, Chaplain, Family Readiness, Counseling

You held space for people in crisis. You made decisions when emotions were high and stakes were real. You built trust in minutes with strangers.

Instead of saying... Say this...
"I provided medical care to service members" "I made critical decisions about people's wellbeing while maintaining their trust in the process"
"I counseled families during deployment" "I guided people through the most stressful periods of their lives — helping them see clearly when everything felt uncertain"
"I triaged under pressure" "I assessed complex situations quickly, prioritized what mattered most, and communicated clearly to people who were scared"
Practice Question Tell me about a time someone trusted you with something that really mattered to them — and how you handled that trust.

The Intelligence / Strategy Analyst

Intelligence, Cyber, Strategic Planning, Research

You synthesized large volumes of ambiguous data into actionable intelligence. You briefed commanders on risk. You thought in scenarios and contingencies.

Instead of saying... Say this...
"I produced intelligence assessments" "I turned incomplete, sometimes contradictory information into clear recommendations that leaders used to make high-stakes decisions"
"I briefed flag officers" "I regularly presented complex analysis to senior decision-makers and defended my reasoning under direct challenge"
"I conducted threat analysis" "I assessed risk across multiple dimensions and built contingency plans — the same mindset you need for comprehensive financial planning"
Practice Question Tell me about a time your analysis changed someone's decision — and what was at stake if you'd gotten it wrong.

Three Rules for Every Answer

01
Lead with the human, not the operation

Every answer should start with a person — a subordinate, a commander, a family member, a patient — and what was at stake for them. Operations are context. People are the story.

Example "One of my team leads was struggling with..." not "During Operation Enduring Freedom, our battalion was tasked with..."
02
Translate in real time

If you say "Battalion S-3," immediately follow with "— the officer responsible for all training and operations planning for 800 people." Never let jargon sit unexplained. The interviewer won't ask you to clarify. They'll just stop listening.

Example "As the S-3 — essentially the COO for an 800-person organization — I was responsible for..."
03
Connect the last 10 seconds to financial advising

End every answer with a bridge. The interviewer shouldn't have to make the connection between your military experience and this role. You make it for them.

Example "That's the same discipline I'd bring to helping families build a financial plan" or "Which is why when I think about helping someone navigate a major financial decision..."

The Honest Truth About Year One

Financial advising is essentially entrepreneurship with a support system. You're building your own practice. That means:

The first 1–2 years are hard.

You're building a client base from scratch. Most of your time will be spent prospecting — calling people, networking, asking for referrals.

Income is variable early on.

Unlike military pay, your compensation will fluctuate. Plan for this financially before you start.

The system works if you work it.

The firms that hire veterans have training systems that have been refined over decades. The people who succeed are the ones who trust the system and do the work — even when it's uncomfortable.

Isolation is a risk.

Military life is deeply communal. If you're in a solo or small-office setup, you need to actively build your support network. Come into the office. Find a mentor. Don't go it alone.

Your military experience is your superpower — but only if you use it.

The discipline, the resilience, the ability to keep going when things are hard — that's what separates the advisors who make it from the ones who don't.
